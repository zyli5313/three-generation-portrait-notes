Three Generations, One Frame: Planning a Grandparents Portrait

A picture of three generations together is one of the few photos families keep for decades. It is also one of the hardest to take, because the people in it rarely live in the same place, and the oldest members may not travel easily. Combining separate photos into one portrait solves the logistics. What remains is planning, and that is what this page covers.

Decide who is in the picture

Start with the people, not the scene. Write down every person who should appear and group them by household. A typical three-generation portrait has two grandparents, two or three adult children, their partners, and the grandchildren. That adds up quickly. If your list goes past eight people, consider two portraits: one of the grandparents with their children, and one of the grandparents with all the grandchildren. Both are valued gifts, and each face stays large enough to recognise.

Remember the pets. In many families the dog is as much a part of the household as anyone. A portrait can usually hold a small number of pets alongside the people, so ask each household whether they want theirs included.

Collect one good photo per person

Each person needs one clear, recent photo of their own face. Group photos where everyone is small do not work well as a source; neither do photos with sunglasses, heavy filters, or faces turned sideways. Send each household a short request:

  • One photo per person, taken this year.
  • Face the camera, in daylight from a window if possible.
  • Phone at eye height, standard lens, no zoom.
  • Send the original file, not a screenshot.

For grandparents, ask a grandchild to take the picture during a visit or a video call. Older relatives often dislike posing, and a familiar face behind the camera helps.

Choose where everyone sits

The traditional arrangement still works best. Grandparents sit in the centre of the front row. Their adult children stand behind them. Grandchildren sit or kneel at the sides of the front row, the youngest closest to the grandparents. Partners stand next to their own family members. Pets sit on the floor in front. This layout puts the oldest faces at the visual centre and keeps households together, which is how families tend to read a portrait.

Pick a setting the grandparents will like

The setting should suit the people being honoured. A garden party or an autumn park feels relaxed; a classic studio backdrop looks formal and timeless; a cosy Christmas living room makes the picture a holiday keepsake. Check the result with the family in mind

Before sharing, look at each face and ask whether the person's spouse would recognise them instantly. Grandparents' faces matter most: wrinkles, glasses and hair should look like them, not a younger version. If a face looks wrong, the fix is almost always a better source photo. Turning it into a gift

A three-generation portrait makes a strong gift for a birthday, an anniversary or the holidays. Print it at a size that suits the wall where it will hang, and include the date and the names on the back. Many families send a digital copy to every household at the same time so nobody feels left out.

Summary

List the people and pets, keep the count manageable, collect one clear photo each, plan the seating with grandparents at the centre, choose a setting they will like, and check every face before you share.
